# Swing Sequence Model

## Sequence

Yeah — I actually like where your thinking has gotten. You're no longer treating the golf swing as "remember 15 corrections while swinging." You're building a repeatable motion sequence, which is much more useful.

I'd clean up your five-step model a little, though, because there are two places where I think you could accidentally create another problem.

---

## Your swing model

### 1. Address — Build the space

This is your foundation. Feet give you enough stability and room to rotate; posture gives your torso and arms room to move; arms hang naturally; clubface is square; grip supports that square face.

The thought is simply:

"Do I have room to swing?"

For the driver especially, you don't want to feel crowded.

### 2. Charge — Turn and create width

This is what you were describing with the torso/shoulder rotation and extended lead arm.

You're loading into the backswing rather than just picking the club up with your arms. Your torso turns, your arms gain depth, and you create that big "hula-hoop" swing plane we've been talking about.

I like charge as your word for this.

One caution: don't manufacture a perfectly straight/locked lead arm. Think wide, not rigid.

### 3. Drop — Get onto the plane

This is probably the most important discovery you've made given your old -12° club path.

Instead of immediately ripping your shoulders/arms toward the ball from the top, you give the arms and club a moment to fall into position as the lower body begins the transition.

That's what helped you find those swings around -2°.

Your cue could literally be:

"Charge → drop."

And I wouldn't consciously think about "releasing the club" yet. The release should increasingly become a result of sequencing, rotation and your wrists/club responding—not a hand-flip that you time to a hip snap.

### 4. Turn — Rotate through the strike

This is where I'd modify what you said.

You mentioned keeping the arms straight and pointing them at the target. That's okay as a feel, but don't actually try to steer your arms down the target line.

Instead, let your body rotation carry the club through.

Chest rotating. Belt buckle eventually facing the target. Trail heel coming up naturally. Arms extending through the strike.

Think:

"Drop → turn through."

That distinction matters because deliberately pushing your hands toward the target can eventually mess with your path and face control.

### 5. Finish — Balance is your checksum

This is the piece I'd add to your model.

Don't just hit the ball and consider the swing finished.

Hold your finish.

You should be able to stand there comfortably with most of your pressure on the lead side, torso facing the target, and no feeling that you're falling forward/backward.

That gives you an immediate diagnostic:

Could I hold that finish for three seconds?

If not, something earlier in the chain probably got rushed.

---

## Additional Notes

And your comment about rising up is worth bringing to your instructor. I wouldn't personally prescribe "stay still" yet. Some vertical movement is normal in a powerful golf swing. What matters is when and why you're changing height. If you're standing up early to create room for the club, that's very different from normal extension through impact.

Your upcoming lesson structure makes sense too. I'd make lesson #1 a true baseline evaluation rather than showing up trying to demonstrate your new swing. Tell the instructor about the old -12° path, the swings you've managed around -2°, your longer-club right miss, and that you've been experimenting with sequencing. Then let them evaluate what is actually causing it.

And I really like your practice philosophy:

Practice isn't "Did I hit good shots today?"

It's "How disciplined was I about practicing the movement I'm trying to own?"

That means you can hit an ugly ball while rehearsing the correct change and still have a productive practice session.

Also, the Nelly Korda aspiration makes sense aesthetically — effortless-looking speed, balance, width, sequencing — but don't try to copy her exact positions. Your proportions and natural movement are different. Steal the principles, not the geometry.

---

## Summary

So if we boil everything you've discovered down to something you can actually remember standing over a ball:

**SPACE → CHARGE → DROP → TURN → BALANCE**

That's your current five-part swing model.

And now that you've found ~-2° at least a few times, the project isn't really "Can Zay make a good swing?" anymore.

It's "Can Zay make that swing boringly repeatable?"
